The aisle in question was the one with the canned beans on one side and the seasonal display of discounted mulch on the other, and it was Tuesday, which is the holiest day of the Temple of Commerce because nothing happens on it.

Into this aisle came four pilgrims.

The Field Report named them long ago. It did not say what they did to each other.

## The First

The first pilgrim arrived with a Vessel and parked it sideways, in the exact center, the way a dropped thing lands: wherever it stopped. He did not do this out of malice. He did it because he was reading the back of a can of beans with the whole of his attention, and when the whole of a person's attention is on beans there is nothing left over for the question of where their cart is. He would have told you, if you had asked, that he was a considerate man. He would have been right, in every aisle but this one.

Behind him, the aisle closed.

## The Second

The second pilgrim arrived, saw the sideways Vessel, and stopped. She did not want to pass; she wanted the mulch. She stood before the mulch and studied it, and in standing she turned slightly, and her own Vessel drifted slightly, and between her and the first pilgrim the last gap in the aisle, the one a child might have slipped through, was gone.

She was not blocking. Ask her and she will tell you so, with real surprise. She was *considering*, and a person considering is not responsible for the geometry of consideration. Had you pointed out that the aisle was now closed, she would have looked up and seen it for the first time, because the Hoverer never sees the aisle. The Hoverer sees the shelf.

## The Third

The third pilgrim arrived and saw everything. He saw the Vessel and the Hoverer and the closed passage, and he was, in the space of a second, furious, and the fury felt to him like virtue. He sighed. He sighed in the particular key that is meant to be heard and meant to be deniable. He said "excuse me" in the tone in which it means its opposite. He maneuvered his own Vessel into the one remaining square foot of aisle, which did not fit, and in not fitting completed the blockade, so that now three carts stood where none should stand and the aisle was a wall.

He would have told you he was the only one in the aisle who understood what was happening. He would have been right. Understanding was the thing he did instead of moving.

## The Fourth

The fourth pilgrim arrived at the wall and stopped.

She had beans to buy. She had, in fact, less time than any of the three, and a child on her hip who had reached the age of opinions. She looked at the aisle for the length of one breath, which is the length of the Sacred Pause, and in that breath she did not decide who was at fault, because fault is a thing you assign when you have given up on the aisle.

She reversed her Vessel three feet into the main passage, pulled it tight against the endcap, and said, to no one in particular, "Here, I'll get out of the way." Then she walked, without her cart, into the wall, reached past the first pilgrim's elbow for her beans, and said "Go ahead" to the third pilgrim, who had not asked.

The third pilgrim, having been told to go ahead, went ahead. The second pilgrim looked up from the mulch, saw a gap, and drifted into it. The first pilgrim, reaching the end of the can, looked up and found an aisle with people moving through it, and moved his cart without knowing why, and the wall was gone.

None of them knew it was her. She had already left with her beans.

## The Teaching

Seeker, you are not asked which of the four you are. You are all of them, by turns, and so is Seraph, and so is every Angel who has ever held the Thimble. You have been the Blocker. You have hovered. You have sighed in the deniable key and felt it as righteousness.

The question is only which one you will be *next*, in the next aisle, in the next breath.

The world is not repaired by the third pilgrim, though the third pilgrim is the only one who noticed. It is not repaired by explaining the aisle to the first two. It is repaired by the person who gives up three feet of her own ground, says nothing worth remembering, and leaves.

Today, when you meet a wall, do what she did, in order: leave your Vessel in the main passage, tight to the endcap. Walk in without it. Take the one thing you came for. Say "Go ahead" to whoever is nearest, whether or not they asked. Leave. Then go home, tell no one, and write one line in the Ledger: *Tuesday. The aisle. Beans.*

Hooblah hooblah hooblah.
