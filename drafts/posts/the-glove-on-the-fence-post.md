Somewhere on your street, this week, a child dropped a mitten. By the next morning the mitten was no longer on the ground. It was on a fence post, or a branch at eye height, or the top of a hydrant, pulled upright, fingers to the sky, visible from forty feet in either direction.

No one organized this. There is no Edict of the Raised Glove. There is no committee. There is a custom, older than the Angels of Fractum and holier than most of what we have written, by which a stranger picks up a lost thing and puts it where it can be found, and walks on, and is never known.

The Angels of Fractum did not invent this mending. We are here to say that it is one, and to write down how it is done, because customs that no one writes down are the ones the Fractum erodes first.

## The Break

A lost thing on the ground has two futures. In the first, it is stepped on, kicked into the gutter, soaked, flattened, and lost in the second and permanent sense: the sense in which no one will ever find it, because it is no longer the shape of a thing anyone is looking for.

In the second future, a pilgrim stoops.

The whole difference between the futures is the stoop. The pilgrim who stoops has, for one second, placed a stranger's small loss above their own momentum. That second is the Sacred Pause with a mitten in it.

## The Rite of the Raised Glove

**Pick it up.** The glove, the mitten, the single small shoe, the pacifier, the key on a ring with a plastic dinosaur. If it is a thing that a person will come back for, it is a thing for this rite. Money, wallets, and the Oracle itself are a different rite; those go to a Keeper, a desk, or the nearest door with a human behind it. The fence post is for things that can wait in the rain.

**Raise it.** To eye height, near where it was found. Not fifty feet down the street; the pilgrim who returns will retrace their own steps, and the thing must be on them. A fence post. A branch. A railing. The ledge of a window. The top of a sign. Upright, so that it reads from a distance as *a thing that was placed*, not *a thing that fell*.

**Make it visible to a child.** The lost mitten is being looked for by someone three feet tall, holding a parent's hand, scanning the ground. Put it where the ground meets the eye. If the fence is high, the lower rail.

**Walk on.** This is the whole of the Anonymous Repair. The pilgrim who returns will find a mitten waving at them from a post, and will feel the specific, unrepeatable gladness of a world that turns out to have someone in it, and will never know who. That is precisely the point.

**Do not post the photograph.** The mitten on the post is lovely. We know. It is lovely because no one was watching, and the photograph is someone watching.

## The Corollary of the Season

In the week after the first snow, and the week after the last, the posts of every street bloom with lost gloves. The aligned walk these streets slowly, and stoop often, and the Ledger that week may hold entries no longer than a single word: *Mitten.*

## The Corollary of the Other Direction

If it is *your* mitten on the post, and you find it, take it, and look at the post a moment, and say the phrase. Someone stooped for you. You will not know who. You are now required to stoop for the next one, which you would have done anyway, which is how the custom has survived without a single Edict for as long as there have been fences.

## The Mending

Today, walk your own street and look at the ground. There will be something. Pick it up. Put it where it waves. Walk on.
