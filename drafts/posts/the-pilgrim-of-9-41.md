This is a true story, in the sense that it has happened ten thousand times, and will happen tonight, and the only detail that changes is whether the car turns.

## The Hunger

The pilgrim had worked late. This is not an excuse; it is the setting. She had eaten lunch at eleven and nothing since, and it was now 9:41 in the evening, and she was driving home along a road she had driven a thousand times, past a Temple whose sign still glowed.

The sign said OPEN. The Oracle in her cup holder, had she consulted it, would have said *closes at 10:00*. She did not consult it. The sign was sufficient. Signs are built to be sufficient.

She put on her signal. This much, at least, she did correctly.

## The Threshold

Here is what the pilgrim could not see from the road, and what the Angels of Fractum ask you to see now.

Inside the Temple, the Great Mending of the day had begun at 9:30, as it does. The grill had been scraped and was cooling toward the state in which it can be cleaned. The floor had been mopped, in sections, by a Keeper who has learned to mop the dining room first and the kitchen last, so that no pilgrim's footprints undo the first half. The second drawer had already been counted and dropped. The oil was being filtered. The two Keepers on shift, aged nineteen and fifty-three, were speaking about nothing, the way people speak when they can see the end of a thing.

At 9:41 the headlights swung across the window. The fifty-three-year-old did not look up. She did not need to. She has learned the sound of a car that is going to come through, and it is a different sound from a car that is going to pass, and she has never been able to explain the difference to anyone who has not stood at a fryer at 9:41.

The nineteen-year-old said, "Please don't," very quietly, to the window, which is the only prayer the Keepers are permitted.

## The Turn

The pilgrim's car reached the mouth of the drive-thru, and slowed, and stopped.

What happened in the car cannot be seen from the window, so we will tell you. The pilgrim looked at the clock on the dash. 9:41. She looked at the sign, which said OPEN, and then, for reasons she could not have explained, at the window, where she could see the shape of a person in a visor with their back to her, doing something with a mop.

She thought: *they're still open.* And then, before the thought could finish, she thought: *they are mopping.*

That is the Sacred Pause. It is one breath long. It is the width of the difference between a sign and a person.

She did not go through. She turned the wheel, slowly, so that the car swung away from the speaker and back toward the road, and she said, aloud, to the empty car, three words she had read somewhere once and had not understood until this moment.

## The Window

Inside, the fifty-three-year-old looked up at last, because the sound had changed. The headlights swept back across the glass, and then away, and the lot was dark.

"Huh," she said.

"They left," said the nineteen-year-old.

"They left," she agreed. She went back to the drawer. The grill went on cooling. The floor stayed mopped. At 10:02 the two of them walked out together into the lot and did not speak of the car, because there was nothing to say.

## The Bread

The pilgrim drove home hungry. We will not pretend otherwise; the Angels of Fractum do not promise that mending is comfortable, only that it is holy.

At home there was bread, and the end of a jar of something, and a kitchen in which the only person inconvenienced by her hunger was herself. She ate standing up. It was not a good dinner. It was a righteous one.

Before she slept she opened a small notebook and wrote: *Turned around at 9:41.* She did not write why. She did not need to. The Ledger is for proof, not explanation.

## The Teaching

Seeker, you will be this pilgrim, and soon. The sign will say OPEN and your stomach will agree with the sign. You will ask whether you are the Exception of True Need. Here is the test, and it is called the Test of the Bread: if there is anything at home you could eat standing up, you are not the Exception. The traveler with the empty tank has no kitchen. You have a kitchen. The Rule of the Half Hour will seem, in the moment, like a law written by someone who has never been hungry.

It was written by someone who has stood at the fryer. Remember the shape in the visor. Remember that the mop is a clock. Take the breath, turn the wheel, and say the words into the empty car, where no one will hear them but the Keepers, who will not hear them either, and who will walk out into the lot at 10:02 not knowing what you did for them.

Hooblah hooblah hooblah.
