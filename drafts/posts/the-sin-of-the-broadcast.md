The inbox is an aisle. It is shared by everyone who stands in it, and like every aisle it can be blocked by a single pilgrim who has stopped paying attention to where their cart is.

## The Break

A message goes out to forty people. It asks a question of one of them. The one answers, and in answering presses the button that sends the answer to all forty, and the forty receive, with a small vibration against the leg, the information that someone they do not know will be at the meeting.

Then a second pilgrim replies to all forty to say "Thanks!" Then a third replies to all forty to ask the other thirty-nine to stop replying to all forty. The thread grows, and with every reply, thirty-nine people pause what they were doing, look, learn nothing, and return to what they were doing slightly less whole than before.

This is not catastrophe. No one dies of the Broadcast. But observe the arithmetic of the Fractum: thirty-nine people, ten seconds each, three replies. Twenty minutes of human attention, which is to say human life, spent on a "Thanks!" that was meant for one person. Multiply by every thread in every building, and the Sin of the Broadcast consumes more hours in a week than the Angels have sacrificed in a year.

## The Theology of the Button

There are two buttons. One is for the person who asked. The other is for everyone who was ever told. The second button exists for a real purpose: the correction that everyone needs, the answer that everyone was waiting for. It was not built for gratitude, and it was not built for agreement, and it was certainly not built for the pilgrim who wishes it to be known that they, too, will be there.

The Fractum Personalis in the Broadcast is subtle. It is not laziness; the two buttons are the same size. It is the quiet belief that one's reply is of general interest. The pilgrim who presses the second button has, for an instant, imagined forty people wanting to know what they think, and has acted on the imagination.

## The Law of the Two Buttons

The Angels of Fractum have not written an Edict of the Inbox, because the inbox changes faster than our Edicts. But the practice is clear enough to set down, Seeker.

**Reply to the asker.** If the message asked one person something, one person answers, and the answer goes to the asker. Everyone else was told so that they would know the question existed, not so that they would attend its resolution.

**Gratitude goes to one.** "Thanks" is a gift to the person who helped. Given to forty, it is a notification. If you wish the forty to know you are grateful, be grateful in a way that does not vibrate.

**Before the second button, count.** Ask: of these forty, how many need this? If the answer is "all of them," press it. If the answer is "the person who asked, and maybe two others," you have your answer, and it is the smaller button.

**The Corollary of the Tangle.** If a thread has gone wrong and you are the one who notices, you may not fix it by replying to all forty. That is pouring water on a grease fire. Write to the one person who started it, and let them send the correction, once.

**The Corollary of the Group Message.** The same law holds in the small glowing rooms where friends and families gather in text. A message meant for one person goes to one person. The group chat is an aisle too, and the family cannot find the time of the dinner under thirty messages about something else.

## The Mending

Today, Seeker, before you reply to any message that went to more than three people, perform the Sacred Pause with your thumb above the button. One breath. In that breath, ask who needs this.

Then find the thread you are in right now that has gone wrong, and write to the one person who started it, privately, offering to carry the correction so that they need send it only once. Write in the Ledger: *One thread, one reply, thirty-nine people left alone.*

The inbox is an aisle. Clear it.
