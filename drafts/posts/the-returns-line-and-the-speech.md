At the back of every Temple of Commerce, past the sacred aisles and the lanes, there is a counter where the pilgrim brings what did not work. A Keeper stands behind it. A line stands before it. And every pilgrim in that line is carrying two things: an item, and a speech.

The Keeper asked for the item.

## The Speech

The sweater was a gift. The giver meant well; the giver always means well; the giver has not seen the pilgrim since a wedding at which the pilgrim was thinner. The color was fine. It was the neck, or the way the neck sat, or the fact that the pilgrim already owns one very like it, also a gift, from the same giver, and so there is a pattern here, and the pilgrim would like the Keeper to appreciate the pattern.

The Keeper has, by now, scanned the receipt.

The returns counter is a confessional that no one sanctioned. The pilgrim has arrived with a small guilt, the guilt of rejecting a thing someone chose, and has mistaken the Keeper for the one who can lift it. She cannot. She is paid by the hour and empowered to issue store credit. Absolution is not in the system.

And so the pilgrim confesses anyway, at length, to a person who needs three things and has already received two of them.

## What the Keeper Can Use

The Keeper's screen has a field for the reason. The field is a menu. It has perhaps six entries, and none of them is "the giver has not seen me since the wedding." The Keeper will hear the whole history of the sweater and select "did not fit," which you could have said in one second of the nine minutes you have spent at the counter.

She can use three things.

- The receipt, or the Oracle's record of it, open before you reach the counter.

- The item, with its tags, in its bag, as it came.

- One word. Fit. Wrong. Duplicate. Changed my mind. The menu does not judge, and neither does she.

Everything beyond these three is a gift to yourself, paid for by someone else.

## The Nine Behind You

Turn, once, Seeker, and look.

Nine pilgrims. Each with an item. Each with a speech as long and as justified as yours, and each waiting while you deliver yours. The pilgrim directly behind you has a toaster and a parking meter. None of them will say anything. Their silence is the Keeper's silence, and you are spending both.

The Sighing Martyr is not the greater Fractum here. You are. He is only the one who noticed.

## The Exception of the Faulty Thing

Let it be said plainly: a thing that is genuinely broken deserves a full account.

If the handle came off in your hand, say so. If the seam opened on the first wearing, say when. If the device does nothing when plugged in, say that, and what you tried. The Keeper needs this. It goes in a different field, and may spare the next pilgrim the same thing.

But a full account is not a long one. It is the three things, then four sentences, then your silence. The fault is in the item. It does not need your biography to explain it.

## The Return That Is Not Made

A further mending happens before you leave the house.

Hold the item. Ask the one question the returns counter cannot process: is this thing wrong, or is it merely unwanted?

A wrong thing goes back. An unwanted thing fits, works, and was chosen by someone thinking of you; its only fault is that you would have chosen otherwise. That is not a defect. That is the ordinary shape of being given something.

The Calendar sets aside one day for this, the day after the winter feast, when the Angel returns nothing at all. But the day is a drill; the practice is the other three hundred and sixty-four. An unwanted thing can be kept, or worn once to the giver's table, or carried to the donation bin, where it will be exactly right for someone who will never need to explain why.

Each of these is a return that is not made, and a Keeper who never hears about the wedding.

## Today's Practice

If you have a return to make, make it like this:

- Before you leave the house, put the receipt, the tags, and the item in one bag.

- Choose your one word in the car.

- At the counter, hand over the three things and say the word. Then stop. If the Keeper asks a question, answer it. If she does not, the silence is the gift.

- If the item is faulty, give the full account, briefly, then the same silence.

- And if, holding the item at home, you find that nothing is wrong with it but you, consider the closet, the giver's table, or the donation bin. The returns counter will not miss you.
