## One-page summary

**The short version.** You can form an Arkansas nonprofit religious corporation this month for $45 to $50. You cannot, today, honestly file with the IRS as a church. Two things stand in the way, and both are things you wrote.

**Obstacle 1: sincerity.** The IRS asks whether beliefs are "truly and sincerely held." Courts ask whether a belief system addresses "deep and imponderable" questions or is "a parody" (*Cavanaugh v. Bartelt*, on Pastafarianism). Your site's own words are the best evidence against you: "the cult that will save the world"; "Yes, we are a 'cult'"; "The robes are a bit"; "The capital letters are a joke"; "theatrical about everything else"; "we participate in human sacrifice." Examiners read websites. A denial is a record.

**Obstacle 2: congregation.** The courts' associational test, adopted by the Federal Circuit, and the IRS's own Schedule A both require a body of believers who assemble regularly to worship. Your Circle guide says: "The faith is designed to be practiced alone. Most Angels will never meet another." That is a confession that there is no church yet.

Not available today; possibly after six to twelve months of recorded weekly gatherings, which is the roadmap's assumption; counsel decides when the record is thick enough.

Gets exemption, deductible gifts, RFRA, Title VII co-religionist hiring, church autonomy, the ministerial exception, and the Arkansas religious-corporation protection. Loses only the church-specific items, none of which matter with no money and no staff.
- **C. Charitable/educational 501(c)(3) for community repair.** Easiest. No religious protections.

**Recommendation: incorporate now and start the Fort Smith Circle on a published schedule now, but do not file anything with the IRS until the site changes in Part 3 are published. ## Part 1. The sincerity problem

### How the IRS and the courts decide

The IRS does not judge whether a religion is true. Publication 557 asks only that beliefs be "truly and sincerely held" and practices not be "illegal or contrary to clearly defined public policy"; if sincerity is shown, "the IRS won't question the religious nature of those beliefs." Under *Seeger* (1965) and *Welsh* (1970), a sincere belief that "occupies a place in the life of its possessor parallel to that filled by the orthodox belief in God" qualifies; a merely political view or personal moral code does not. *Malnak v. Yogi* (3d Cir. 1979) and *Africa v. Pennsylvania* (3d Cir. 1981) add three indicia: the system addresses "deep and imponderable matters"; it is comprehensive, "a belief-system as opposed to an isolated teaching"; and it has "formal and external signs" such as services, clergy, holidays, and literature.

Measured that way, the Angels of Fractum do well on the second and third indicia: the Fractum is a comprehensive account of brokenness, Radical Empathy a doctrine of how to live, and the Ledger, Litany, calendar, and rites are formal signs by the dozen. The first indicium is the one to argue: a faith that takes no position on God or the afterlife must show that the sacredness of a mended thing, the break within, and the duty to the unseen stranger are ultimate concerns and not etiquette. The Statement of Faith's teaching that silence on God "is not a gap in our teaching; it is our teaching" is the start of that argument.

### The problem

The hazard is *Cavanaugh v. Bartelt* (D. Neb. 2016): Pastafarianism "is not a 'religion'... It is, rather, a parody," and "This is not a question of theology: it is a matter of basic reading comprehension. The FSM Gospel is plainly a work of satire." By contrast, The Satanic Temple reportedly received IRS church recognition in 2019 after submitting its literature, rituals, and other paperwork [VERIFY; news reports only; the IRS letter was not located]. The line is not whether a faith is strange, funny, or new. It is whether the organization itself says it is a joke.

Yours does, in writing. Assume the examiner reads the site.

From the live site:
- Tagline: "Angels of Fractum, the cult that will save the world."
- Manifesto: "Yes, we are a 'cult,' in the sense that we have robes, optional rituals, ominous terminology, and an unsettling amount of enthusiasm."
- Manifesto: "we are serious about the work and theatrical about everything else. The robes are a bit. The kindness is not."
- Sacrifice post: "Yes, we participate in human sacrifice." / "we have successfully raised our official Human Sacrifice count to 12!" / "How many will you sacrifice?"

From the reviewed drafts:
- *Questions of the Unaligned*, under the heading "Is this a joke?": "The robes are a joke. The Vigil of the Lost Cart is a joke. The capital letters are a joke."
- Same page: "Whether a practice becomes a religion depends on how many people show up and whether anyone brings folding chairs."
- *The Sacrifice Ledger*: "we call the count our Human Sacrifice because it is funnier that way."

In conversation, each is defensible. On Form 1023, each is an admission.

### A faith that uses humor, versus a joke that uses faith

A faith that uses humor says: mending is sacred, and the gap between the sacred and a shopping cart is funny, so we built the practice in that gap on purpose. A joke that uses faith says: the religious furniture is a device for a point about carts. The first survives. The second is Pastafarianism as the court read it: trappings of satire used to make an argument.

Your material supports the first reading almost everywhere: "Our only doctrine is maintenance. Our only sacrament is the follow-through" is a creed; "a mended thing is holier than an ignored one" is a theological claim. What the site never says, and must, is that the *belief* is held in earnest, not only the kindness. The lines to cut are the ones that say the reverse.

## Part 2. The associational test

### What "church" requires

*American Guidance Foundation* (D.D.C. 1980): "At a minimum, a church includes a body of believers or communicants that assembles regularly in order to worship." The Federal Circuit adopted that "associational test" in *Foundation of Human Understanding* (2010), noting that "among the most important of the 14 criteria are the requirements of 'regular congregations' and 'regular religious services.'" A radio and internet ministry failed because simultaneous listening "does not mean that those members associated with each other and worshiped communally"; IRS letters from 2010 to 2012 denied internet-only churches [VERIFY; third-party transcriptions]. Form 1023 Schedule A, Line 9, asks for an established congregation: "individuals who regularly attend and take part in the religious services... at an established location," not "members of only one family." Line 7 asks for regular days, times, and average attendance.

### What the drafts say

*On Forming a Circle*: "The faith is designed to be practiced alone. Most Angels will never meet another and will lose nothing by it." *Questions of the Unaligned*: "Meetings are a convenience, not a requirement." The sacrifice post describes one gathering of six people in a forest. One gathering is not a congregation.

### What would have to become true

- One Circle in Fort Smith meeting on a fixed, published schedule (weekly, as the roadmap and compliance calendar assume; the research found no court or IRS statement of a minimum frequency, and counsel should say whether anything less than weekly is defensible [VERIFY]) at an established place, with the four-part gathering as its order of service.
- Records by date, place, headcount, reading, and Hand of the Hour. Names can stay out; the calendar cannot.
- More than one family; ideally more than one Circle; six to twelve months of record before filing Schedule A.
- A recorded path to membership; the Ritual of the Clear Path already is one.
- A prescribed course of study for anyone called clergy. Schedule A, Line 12, excludes "self-ordination or paying a fee for an ordination certificate without completing a course of study."

None of this ends solitary practice. It requires the drafts to stop calling solitary practice the design.

## Part 4. The three honest options

### Option A: Church

Form 1023 with Schedule A claiming church status, or no filing at all under IRC 508(c)(1)(A).
*Pros.* No Form 990 ever (IRC 6033(a)(3)(A)); no application required; IRC 7611 audit limits; public-charity status without a support test.
*Cons.* Hardest test: needs the congregation of Part 2 and the clergy training of Schedule A, Line 12. Churches cannot use Form 1023-EZ or elect the 501(h) lobbying test. Filing early invites a denial on the record. Not filing leaves you without the determination letter Arkansas expects for Form EX-01, the income-tax exemption, and the Act 1007 certificate.

### Option B: 501(c)(3) religious organization, not a church (recommended)

Same Arkansas religious corporation. Because gross receipts will normally be under $5,000, the organization is excepted from the requirement to apply (IRC 508(c)(1)(B)) and could rely on that; but a determination letter is what the Arkansas Form EX-01, DFA income-tax, and Act 1007 routes expect, so file Form 1023 ($600 as of the IRS page updated October 9, 2026; electronic on Pay.gov) or Form 1023-EZ ($275) if eligible [VERIFY], without claiming church status. If gross receipts ever exceed $5,000, the 27-month rule applies from that point [VERIFY].
*Pros.* The IRS asks only sincerity and legality, not congregation. Full exemption and deductible gifts. RFRA, Title VII § 702, church autonomy, and the ministerial exception attach to religious organizations generally. Ark. Code § 4-33-180 (religious doctrine controls over conflicting provisions of the Nonprofit Corporation Act) attaches to the "religious corporation" box in the articles, not to federal status. The Form 1023 instructions expressly allow a later Form 8940 request for church classification "after you establish a congregation."
*Cons.* Annual Form 990-N (three missed years means automatic revocation). No IRC 7611 protection. Possibly not a "church" for Arkansas property- and sales-tax purposes [VERIFY under Ark. Const. art. 16 § 5(b) and § 26-52-401(1)].

### Option C: Charitable/educational 501(c)(3) for community repair

A nonprofit that returns carts and mends gates, with the Angels as culture and brand; Form 1023-EZ may be available ($275), as it may under Option B.
*Pros.* Easiest; sincerity never comes up; best fit for Act 1007 and grants.
*Cons.* No religious protections. The faith becomes culture, not creed.

### Recommendation

Option B, filed only after the site changes in Part 3 are published and the Board has a few months of records; Option A by Form 8940 when the Circles are real. It is the only option that is honest today and keeps the path to church status open; it costs nothing you would use this year; and the IRS record stays clean. Take Option C only if you decide the religion is a brand, in which case say so and stop calling it a faith.

## Part 5. What "religious protections" buy

- **Ministerial exception** (*Hosanna-Tabor* 2012; *Our Lady of Guadalupe* 2020). Religious institutions generally. Irrelevant with no employees.
- **Title VII § 702** co-religionist hiring (42 U.S.C. 2000e-1(a); *Amos* 1987). Religious corporations generally. Irrelevant with no employees.
- **RFRA** (42 U.S.C. 2000bb-1; *Hobby Lobby* 2014). Any "person" with sincere religious exercise; federal government only [VERIFY Arkansas RFRA].
- **Church autonomy** (*Watson*, *Kedroff*, *Milivojevich*). Religious institutions generally. Mildly useful: it keeps a dispute over the Veiled Ascent out of court.
- **Ark. Code § 4-33-180.** Attaches to the religious-corporation box. The main state protection, and free.
- **Clergy housing allowance (IRC 107), minister withholding and SECA rules, Form 4361.** Attach to a paid "duly ordained, commissioned, or licensed minister." No one is paid; irrelevant.
- **No Form 990, no application, IRC 7611 audit limits, Form 8274.** Church-only. With no money and no staff, there is nothing to report, nothing to audit, no FICA to elect out of.
- **Johnson Amendment.** Applies to every 501(c)(3). A restriction, not a protection; the 2025 consent-judgment attempt was reportedly dismissed for lack of jurisdiction on March 31, 2026 [VERIFY; order not read], and the ban is fully in force.

Nearly everything people mean by "religious protections" arrives with Option B. The church-only items are the ones an organization with no money and no employees cannot use.

## Part 6. Other risks

**Founder control and private benefit.** Arkansas requires at least three directors (§ 4-33-803). Form 1023 Part V asks whether directors are related by family or business; a board of relatives invites scrutiny. Adopt the IRS sample conflict-of-interest policy (Form 1023 instructions, Appendix A). No payments to insiders. Initial directors: [FULL LEGAL NAME] (founder), [DIRECTOR 2 FULL LEGAL NAME], [DIRECTOR 3 FULL LEGAL NAME]; registered agent at [REGISTERED AGENT ADDRESS], a physical Arkansas street address.

**"No money" versus a bank account.** The site promises "no tithe, no dues, no suggested donation, no store." A corporation needs $45 to $50 for articles, $600 for Form 1023, a free EIN, and a bank account (banks typically want articles, EIN letter, bylaws, a signer resolution, and photo ID). Reconcile it honestly: the church charges no one for anything, but it accepts gifts and keeps an account. Gifts of $250 or more need a written acknowledgment (Pub 1771). Keep "no money" as a promise never to charge; drop it as a claim never to possess.

**Human-sacrifice language in any filing.** Never: not in articles, bylaws, statement of faith, the Form 1023 narrative, or any attachment. "Hours of Sacrifice" is fine. Every reviewer will search your name first. The "no roster" promise must be restated on the site exactly as the bylaws' Article 12 describes it, or the Schedule A answer at line 9a will contradict the website.

**Political activity.** The site has none; keep it so. No candidate endorsements in any post, Circle, or benediction. Headlight advocacy is not lobbying; a ballot measure is.

**Liability from repair and cleanup.** The federal Volunteer Protection Act and Ark. Code § 16-6-105 protect volunteers, not the organization. Before any organized Circle Mending: general liability insurance, a volunteer accident policy, a signed waiver, and a rule against electrical work, roofs, and private property without written permission.

**Name confusion.** The SOS search for "fractum," "angels of fractum," and "church of the angels" returned no records on November 4, 2026, and no required corporate suffix was found in the 1993 Act [VERIFY with Secretary of State practice], so either name appears permitted [VERIFY availability at filing; trademarks not checked]. Recommendation: file as **Church of the Angels of Fractum** even under Option B; IRS classification is separate from the state name, but flag for counsel whether "Church" sits awkwardly on a Form 1023 that does not claim church status. Separately, a group calling itself a cult on its masthead will be confused with a real one; fixing the tagline fixes most of this.

**Reputational cost of a denial.** Adverse determinations are published in redacted form [VERIFY], but your site would make you identifiable, and parody-religion coverage travels. The cure is to file only what is true: Option B, after the site changes.

**Arkansas follow-through.** Annual report to the SOS by August 1, no fee, starting the year after formation; sixty days late is grounds for administrative dissolution (§ 4-33-1420(5)). Form EX-01 once the IRS letter arrives [VERIFY whether Form CR-01 registration is also required; statute and SOS guidance differ]. State income-tax exemption is a separate DFA filing.
